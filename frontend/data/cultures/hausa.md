# History

## Overview of Hausa Culture

Hausa culture, known broadly as *al'adun Hausawa*, refers to the customs, traditions, social systems, and ways of life practised by the Hausa people of northern Nigeria and southern Niger. Hausa culture is deeply shaped by Islam, community identity, lineage, and the historical legacies of the Sokoto Caliphate and the precolonial Hausa city-states.

The Hausa are the largest ethnic group in Nigeria and are so intertwined with the Fulani that both groups are frequently counted together as Hausa-Fulani. Although Hausa communities share common cultural foundations, regional variations exist across different states and settlements due to migration, historical experience, and local traditions.

Core values of Hausa culture include:
- Respect for elders and social hierarchy
- Islamic faith and religious observance
- Community and family loyalty
- Hospitality (*karimci*)
- Hard work and trade
- Preservation of lineage and ancestry

## Precolonial Era

In the fourteenth century, Hausaland was formed as a loose confederation of Hausa city-states located in present-day northern Nigeria and southern Niger. These states were not unified under a single ruler but shared language, culture, and trade networks. Malian Muslims introduced Islam to the Hausa people in the late fourteenth century, though observance was initially limited to urban areas while traditional religious practices remained widespread in rural communities.

## The Sokoto Jihad and Its Legacy

The most transformative event in Hausa history was the Fulani Jihad of the early nineteenth century. Led by Shaykh Uthman bn Foduye (Usman dan Fodio), the jihad resulted in the conquest of the Hausa States by the Fulani and the establishment of the Sokoto Caliphate. The Fulani rulers reorganized the conquered states into emirates and instituted Islamic law throughout the region.

The Sokoto Jihad scholars — particularly the triumvirate of Shaykh Uthman bn Foduye, his brother Malam Abdullahi of Gwandu, and his son Sultan Muhammad Bello — produced an extensive body of literature in Arabic, Fulfulde, and Hausa. Their works addressed religion, governance, social life, and the nature of the communities of Hausaland. They wrote with the main objective of educating, enlightening, and guiding the people on various aspects of their political, social, and religious lives.

## British Colonial Era and Independence

The British colonial era began with the annexation of Lagos in 1861. By 1903, the northern Nigerian emirates were under British control, though the British largely maintained the organizational structure and governance practices of the emirates. Nigeria became an independent country on 1 October 1960 but quickly divided along geographic and ethnic lines — the Hausa-Fulani in the north, the Yoruba in the southwest, and the Igbo in the southeast.

Since independence, Nigeria has experienced two periods of military rule — 1966 to 1979, and 1983 to 1999 — before returning to civilian government in 1999.

## The Concept of Hausa Traditional Religion (Maguzanci)

Before the widespread adoption of Islam, and continuing in pockets to this day, the Hausa people practised a traditional religion known as *Maguzanci*. The practitioners of this religion are called *Maguzawa*, and an individual adherent is called a *Bamaguje*.

The term *Maguzanci* refers to the values, conduct, and ways of life of the Maguzawa that are contrary to the teachings of Islam, inherited from their ancestors before the advent of Islam in Hausaland. It was attributed to those original Hausa people who refused to accept Islam when their communities converted.

### The Origin of the Term Maguzawa

The origin of the term *Maguzawa* is a debated issue among scholars. One folk explanation holds that the word was coined after the Fulani and Hausa accepted Islam. According to this tale, a man named Bama refused to accept Islam and distanced himself from those who prayed — the phrase *ya guje* meaning "he ran away" — giving rise to the name Bamaguje (plural: Maguzawa). However, this explanation is widely regarded as a legendary popular etymology rather than historical fact.

A more widely supported view, endorsed by scholars such as Greenberg, holds that *Maguzawa* is derived from the Arabic word *Majus*, meaning Magian or Zoroastrian. According to this view, Hausa Muslim jurists applied this term to the non-Muslim Hausa communities in a manner analogous to how the word *Majus* was used in Islamic jurisprudence to categorize certain non-Muslim groups.

### The Majus and the Maguzawa

Despite the etymological connection, the Sokoto Jihad scholars drew a clear distinction between the *Majus* and the *Maguzawa*. The *Majus* — known in Western scholarship as Zoroastrians — were followers of a Persian pagan dualistic religion that acknowledged two deities: a deity of goodness and a deity of evil, symbolized by light and darkness. They are mentioned once in the Quran alongside Jews and Christians, and classical Islamic scholars categorized them as quasi people of the book (*man lahu shubhatu kitab*).

The Maguzawa Hausa people, by contrast, were described by the Sokoto Jihad scholars as polytheists and pagans — worshippers of stones and spirits who possessed no divine book. Sultan Muhammad Bello, in his work *Infaq al Maisur*, described the Maguzawa as disbelievers who worshipped stones and *Jinn*, neither performed prayer nor observed fasting, and made sacrilegious statements about God. Scholars such as Dr. Adamu Musa Kotorkoshi therefore argue that *Maguzanci* is not an offshoot of the *Majus* religious tradition, and that the Jihad scholars did not equate the Maguzawa with the Majus in legal or theological terms.

### Practices of Maguzanci

According to the Sokoto Jihad literature and contemporary scholarship, Hausa traditional religion involved:
- Spirit worship in the form of fetishism (*tsafi*)
- Spirit possession (*bori*)
- Worship of stones, idols (*gunki*), and jinn
- Superstitious beliefs and syncretism with Islamic practices


# Traditions

## Greetings (*Gaisuwa*)

### Meaning and Description

Greetings are an essential and elaborate part of Hausa social life. It is considered rude to get directly to the purpose of a conversation or meeting without first engaging in proper greetings. Small talk about a person's family, job, or health is expected before any serious discussion begins.

Men typically greet each other with a handshake of the right hand while maintaining the handshake as conversation begins. In northern Nigeria, physical contact between genders is discouraged, so cross-gender handshakes are generally considered inappropriate. Greetings are typically lengthy because it reflects respect and genuine interest in the other person's wellbeing.

Core values in Hausa greetings include:
- Patience and respect
- Inquiry about family and wellbeing
- Humility before elders
- Taking time before addressing the purpose of the meeting

### Common Greetings
- *Yaqaya kakay?* — How are you?
- *Laafeeya kalo* — Fine, very well
- *Barka da raanaa!* — Good afternoon!
- *Zan eyshga masalaachan?* — May I enter the mosque?

## Social Structure

### Meaning and Description

Most of the Hausa population lives in northern Nigeria, and while there are urban settlements such as Kano, the majority of Hausa live in rural communities. Hausa society is hierarchical, with individuals ranked according to family lineage, wealth, occupation, and social achievement. Lineage is recorded and traced through the paternal line.

The Fulani emerged as the aristocracy of northern Nigeria following the Sokoto Jihad of the early nineteenth century. Unlike the Hausa, the Fulani do not maintain as rigid a social hierarchy. The nomadic lifestyle of the Fulani, centered on cattle herding, is regarded as a prestigious occupation and a cultural institution.

Authority is distributed in Hausa society among:
- Emirs and traditional rulers
- Lineage heads and elders
- Religious leaders and Islamic scholars
- Title holders and community leaders
- Family representatives

Important decisions require consultation among recognized authorities. The most senior individual is granted special respect and is expected to make decisions in the best interest of a group. Titles — both traditional and honorary — are important, and people should be addressed using their proper titles as a sign of respect.

## Marriage (*Aure*)

### Meaning and Description

Hausa weddings are based on Islamic marriage traditions. Marriage in Hausa culture is a union between families as much as between individuals. It reinforces lineage, social alliances, and community standing.

Marriage is associated with:
- Responsibility and continuity of ancestry
- Fertility and family growth
- Social status
- Communal acceptance
- Religious duty

### Stages of Hausa Marriage

#### Proposal and Vetting

When a Hausa man proposes, his potential in-laws subject him to an extensive vetting process. The formal marriage arrangement takes place between the parents of the bride and the groom. Families consider moral reputation, social standing, and family conduct.

#### Bride Price (*Sadaki*)

A dowry is negotiated between the two families and paid by the groom's family to the bride's family. A smaller dowry is considered to bring greater blessings to the marriage, so the dowry does not need to be extravagant.

#### Wedding Ceremony

A wedding contract outlining the marriage, naming the witnesses, and listing the dowry is signed. The ceremony itself is a small and quiet affair, usually attended only by close family members and those officiating. A bridal shower typically follows the wedding ceremony.

#### Wedding Reception

The wedding reception is a day-long celebration that usually takes place the day after the ceremony. Hausa wedding traditions are generally less extravagant and shorter than those of the Yoruba or Igbo.

### Cultural Significance

According to Hausa tradition, the husband provides a house for the new family and the wife's family furnishes it. The wedding is seen as a fusion of two families rather than simply the joining of two individuals.

## Divorce (*Saki*)

### Meaning and Description

In northern Nigeria, Islamic law stipulates that a divorce is granted after the husband states to his wife "I divorce you" three times. Nigeria's official divorce statistics do not reflect the actual prevalence of divorce, particularly in the north, because the government does not track religious marriages or divorces. Many divorces therefore go unrecorded.

Divorce can be devastating for women who married young. Traditional and religious marriages do not offer the same legal protections as civil marriages, so divorce can leave women without income or land. Women who divorce and return to their families are often seen as failures. Divorced Nigerian women face difficulty in finding a new spouse. Children typically remain with the father after a divorce.

## Death and Funeral Practices

### Meaning and Description

Hausa funerals are conducted in accordance with Islamic funeral traditions. The funeral takes place within twenty-four hours of death. Before burial, the deceased is washed and shrouded in white sheets. After the body is shrouded, it is prayed over. The funeral service takes place outside the mosque, with participants standing and reciting prayers.

A funeral procession accompanies the body to the gravesite, though women and children are not present at the burial itself. The body is placed into the grave without a coffin, with the head facing toward Mecca. Cremation is considered desecration. Grave markers are modest, and flowers and mementos are not placed at the grave.

Close relatives observe a three-day mourning period. Widows observe a mourning period lasting over four months, during which they refrain from wearing jewelry or perfume and must remain at home at night.

## Community Gatherings and Decision Making

### Meaning and Description

Community gatherings are central to Hausa social life. Important matters affecting families, villages, and lineages are discussed publicly through meetings involving elders, family heads, religious leaders, and titled members of the community. Traditional Hausa society values collective participation. Decisions are rarely made by one person alone because the community is believed to be stronger when people deliberate together.

Gatherings may take place for:
- Marriage discussions
- Settlement of disputes
- Land matters and resource allocation
- Funeral planning
- Festival organization
- Religious consultations

In many communities, the eldest or most senior man speaks first, while younger people listen respectfully before contributing.

### Cultural Significance

Community gatherings strengthen:
- Unity and social cohesion
- Accountability among community members
- Collective identity
- Conflict resolution
- Preservation of tradition

## Festivals (*Bukukuwa*)

### Durbar Festival

The Durbar Festival is celebrated throughout northern Nigeria at the end of Ramadan. Originally an annual demonstration of warrior skills, horsemanship, and an oath of loyalty to the local emir, it has become a celebration of regional culture. The Durbar involves a large procession of local peoples dressed in ceremonial attire, with elaborately dressed horses and riders, traditional dances, and participation by the emir and his entourage.

### Sharo Festival

The Sharo Festival is celebrated by the Fulani as a rite of passage for young men. Held twice a year, it is a demonstration of strength and bravery. The week-long festival includes singing, drumming, skits, and flogging. Male adolescents undergo flogging as they compete for the right to marry a girl of their choosing.

### Argungu Fishing Festival

The Argungu Fishing Festival is a four-day festival held in northwestern Nigeria. Men and boys participate in fishing, hunting, wrestling, and boxing competitions. The festival serves to pass down traditional skills from fathers to sons. Women participate in music and dance performances.

## Gift Giving and Hospitality (*Karimci*)

### Meaning and Description

Hospitality is a core value in Hausa culture. Visitors are welcomed with food and drinks before important discussions begin. Most Nigerians eat with their hands. Men and women eat their meals separately in Muslim households. If invited to a Nigerian household, bringing fruit, nuts, or chocolates is a polite gesture.

Gift giving in Nigeria carries a strong social meaning but has also come under criticism for facilitating patronage networks and corruption. It is important to exercise good judgment when giving or receiving gifts, as they may be perceived as bribes.

## Dos and Don'ts

### Dos
- Show respect for those older than you, even if their professional status is lower
- Ask before taking anyone's photograph
- Lower your eyes when meeting a senior or elder person as a sign of respect
- Address people by their titles as a sign of respect
- Take time with greetings and inquire about the other person's wellbeing

### Don'ts
- Don't use your left hand in dealings with Muslims, as the left hand is considered unclean
- Don't discuss intimate matters in public
- Don't clean your plate completely; leaving a little food indicates you have had enough
- Don't rush greetings
- Don't mistreat religious symbols, especially the Quran


# Culture and Cultural Symbols

## Islam and Religious Life

### Meaning and Description

Islam is the dominant religion among the Hausa people of northern Nigeria. The Pew Research Center estimates that Muslims make up approximately forty-nine percent of Nigeria's total population, and among the Hausa, Islam shapes governance, family structure, dress, daily routine, diet, and social interaction.

The majority of Nigerian Muslims are Sunni, and religious observance is high. Eighty-eight percent of Nigerians say religion is a very important part of their lives.

### The Five Pillars of Islam

Regardless of their specific sect, Muslims follow the five Pillars of Islam:
- *Shahada* — the declaration of faith: "There is no god but God and Muhammad is God's messenger"
- *Salat* — prayer five times daily
- *Sawm* — fasting during Ramadan
- *Zakat* — giving alms and sharing wealth with those in need
- *Hajj* — pilgrimage to Mecca at least once in one's lifetime

### Sharia Law

The Nigerian constitution allows state courts to adopt customary law. As a result, twelve northern states have opted to have Sharia courts with jurisdiction over civil matters involving Muslims. Sharia courts cannot force non-Muslims to appear before them, and secular courts continue to operate alongside them in the same states.

## Care and Treatment of the Quran

Muslims regard the Quran as sacred, and proper treatment of Islam's holy book is important:
- Keep the Quran on the highest shelf of a bookcase as a sign of respect
- Do not touch the Quran with dirty hands
- Do not place the Quran on the floor
- Protect the Quran with a dust cover when not in use
- Do not take a Quran into a latrine
- Old or damaged Qurans may be properly disposed of by respectful burning or by burial

## Hausa Literature (*Adabin Hausa*)

### Meaning and Description

Hausa writing emerged around the fourteenth or fifteenth century. The Hausa language was written in the Arabic script until the British introduced the Latin script in 1903. The Sokoto Jihad scholars produced an enormous body of written works in Arabic, Fulfulde, and Hausa comprising poetry, prose, sermons, and legal treatises. These works addressed religious reform, social organization, and the nature of the communities of Hausaland.

Modern Hausa literature addresses social problems and women's issues. Cultural norms are explored and questioned by contemporary Hausa authors. Many Hausa authors are women, and their work has been well received by the public. Popular themes include marital issues, women's empowerment, and education.

### Historical Significance

The Sokoto Jihad literature represents one of the most substantial bodies of indigenous West African intellectual output. These works are important not only as religious texts but as historical documents that illuminate the social, political, and religious landscape of nineteenth-century Hausaland.

## The Nri Influence and Sacred Authority

Unlike kingdoms that expanded through military conquest, the authority of the Sokoto Caliphate was built substantially on religious legitimacy and scholarly prestige. The Jihad scholars wrote their works with the goal of educating and enlightening communities, demonstrating that moral and intellectual authority can be as powerful a force for social transformation as military strength.

## Age Grades and Social Institutions

### Meaning and Description

Traditional Hausa society organized social life through hierarchical institutions. Although formal age-grade systems are more associated with Igbo culture, Hausa society organizes community responsibility through lineage groups, extended family networks, emirate structures, and Islamic institutions.

Community functions are distributed among:
- Extended family (*iyali*) networks for domestic and social matters
- Emirate councils for governance and law
- Islamic scholars (*ulama*) for religious guidance
- Craft guilds for economic activity
- Market associations for trade

## Markets and Commerce

### Meaning and Description

The Hausa have historically been among the most active traders in West Africa. Kano, the largest city in northern Nigeria, was historically a center of trade connecting sub-Saharan Africa with North Africa and the Middle East via the trans-Saharan trade routes.

Economic and social life revolves around the market. Markets serve as:
- Centers of trade and commerce
- Meeting places for social interaction
- Information exchange points
- Venues for cultural exchange
- Economic lifelines for communities

The Hausa economy today includes manufacturing, agriculture, trade, and crafts, though the northern economy has been significantly damaged by insecurity and the activities of extremist groups.

## Merit and Social Achievement

### Meaning and Description

Traditional Hausa society places value on social mobility through personal achievement, piety, and scholarship. While family background and lineage are important, individuals can improve their social standing through:
- Accumulation of wealth through trade
- Islamic scholarship and religious piety
- Political leadership
- Community service
- Generosity and patronage

A person's reputation depends significantly on their demonstrated achievements and moral conduct. This is reflected in the emphasis the Sokoto Jihad scholars placed on education, piety, and moral reform as the foundations of a just society.


# Festivals (*Emume*)

### Meaning and Description

Festivals are major cultural events that connect the living community with religion, history, and identity. They preserve cultural heritage, celebrate achievements, and reinforce community unity. Hausa festivals often blend Islamic observance with local cultural traditions.

## Eid al-Fitr (*Babban Salla*)

Eid al-Fitr marks the end of Ramadan and is celebrated with feasts and donations to the poor. It is one of the most significant holidays in the Hausa calendar and is accompanied by prayers, communal celebrations, and often the Durbar procession of horses and riders. The Sultan of Sokoto, as the highest-ranking Islamic authority in Nigeria, plays a central role in Eid observances.

## Eid al-Kabir (*Sallah*)

Also known as Eid al-Adha, this festival celebrates the willingness of Ibrahim to sacrifice his son Ishmael in obedience to God. Animals are sacrificed and the meat is distributed among family, neighbors, and the poor. This is one of the most widely celebrated holidays throughout northern Nigeria.

## Eid al-Maulud

The celebration of the Prophet Muhammad's birthday. Sunni Muslims celebrate this holiday five days earlier than Shia Muslims. In Hausa communities, the day is marked with religious gatherings, recitations of prayers, and communal celebrations.

## Durbar Festival

The Durbar is celebrated throughout northern Nigeria at the end of Ramadan and during other major royal occasions. A large procession involving elaborately dressed horses and riders, ceremonial attire, traditional dances, and the participation of the emir and his entourage. Originally a demonstration of warrior skills and loyalty, the Durbar has become one of northern Nigeria's most spectacular cultural events.

## Secular Holidays

Secular national holidays in Nigeria include:
- National Day — 1 October, celebrating Nigeria's 1960 independence from Great Britain
- Workers' Day — 1 May
- Democracy Day — 29 May, commemorating the return to civilian government in 1999
- New Year's Day — 1 January
- Boxing Day — 26 December


# Beliefs

## Religion and Spiritual Beliefs (*Addini da Imani*)

### Meaning and Description

Islam is the primary religion of the Hausa people, and it is deeply integrated into every aspect of Hausa life. The spiritual system centers on belief in one God (Allah), the prophethood of Muhammad, and the authority of the Quran.

The Hausa also maintain a historical awareness of their pre-Islamic religious traditions, known as *Maguzanci*. While the majority of Hausa people today are Muslim, *Maguzanci* continues to influence certain cultural practices and folk beliefs in some communities.

### Traditional Religion (Maguzanci)

The primary forms of traditional Hausa religion include:
- *Tsafi* — fetishism and the worship of sacred objects
- *Bori* — spirit possession rituals in which practitioners enter a trance state to communicate with spirits
- Veneration of spirits associated with natural forces
- Communication with ancestral spirits

The Sokoto Jihad scholars described the Maguzawa as polytheists (*mushrikun*) and disbelievers (*kuffar*) — people who worshipped inanimate and spiritual objects, similar in nature to the pagan Arabs of the pre-Islamic era.

### Islam

Islam is the dominant faith of the Hausa people. The majority of Hausa Muslims are Sunni, following the Maliki school of Islamic law. Islam was introduced to Hausaland by Malian Muslims in the fourteenth century and was strengthened and institutionalized through the Sokoto Jihad of the nineteenth century.

Islamic belief shapes every dimension of Hausa life — from marriage customs and funeral rites to dress, diet, and daily scheduling around the five prayer times.

## Ancestors and Community Memory

### Meaning and Description

Although Islam does not endorse ancestor veneration in the way some other African traditions do, the Hausa maintain a strong sense of historical and genealogical identity. Knowledge of lineage is important, and family reputation passed from ancestors is highly valued.

The Sokoto Jihad scholars preserved community memory through their extensive literary output, and their works remain important resources for understanding the history and religious landscape of Hausaland. The Sultan of Sokoto continues to serve as the highest-ranking Islamic authority in Nigeria, representing both religious leadership and living historical memory.

### Cultural Significance

Ancestral and historical memory reinforces:
- Continuity between generations
- Respect for family lineage and community history
- Moral responsibility and Islamic piety
- Preservation of cultural and religious identity

# Food (*Abinci*)

## Tuwo Shinkafa (Rice Porridge)
**Hausa Name:** Tuwo Shinkafa
**Category:** Staple

### Ingredients
- Rice
- Water
- Salt (optional)

### Preparation
1. Wash the rice thoroughly.
2. Cook the rice in water using more water than usual until very soft.
3. Mash or stir vigorously until a smooth sticky porridge begins to form.
4. Continue cooking and stirring until the mixture becomes thick and stiff.
5. Shape into balls or serve directly.
6. Serve with soup.

## Tuwo Masara (Corn Porridge)
**Hausa Name:** Tuwo Masara
**Category:** Staple

### Ingredients
- Corn flour (finely ground)
- Water
- Salt (optional)

### Preparation
1. Bring water to a boil in a pot.
2. Gradually add the corn flour while stirring continuously.
3. Continue stirring to prevent lumps from forming.
4. Cook over medium heat until the mixture thickens.
5. Stir until a smooth, firm dough forms.
6. Serve hot with soup or stew.

## Miyan Kuka (Baobab Leaf Soup)
**Hausa Name:** Miyan Kuka
**Category:** Soup

### Ingredients
- Baobab leaf powder (kuka)
- Palm oil
- Meat (beef, goat, or dried fish)
- Locust bean seasoning (dawadawa)
- Pepper
- Salt
- Water

### Preparation
1. Cook the meat until tender.
2. Add palm oil and bring to a boil.
3. Add locust bean seasoning, pepper, and salt.
4. Gradually add the baobab leaf powder while stirring.
5. Stir continuously to prevent lumps.
6. Allow to simmer until the soup reaches the desired consistency.
7. Serve with tuwo.

## Miyan Taushe (Pumpkin Soup)
**Hausa Name:** Miyan Taushe
**Category:** Soup

### Ingredients
- Pumpkin (taushe)
- Groundnut paste
- Palm oil
- Meat or dried fish
- Pepper
- Onion
- Seasoning
- Salt
- Water

### Preparation
1. Peel and cut the pumpkin into pieces.
2. Cook the meat until soft.
3. Add palm oil and onion.
4. Add the pumpkin and cook until soft.
5. Mash the pumpkin into the soup.
6. Add groundnut paste and stir well.
7. Add pepper, seasoning, and salt.
8. Simmer until well blended.
9. Serve with tuwo or rice.

## Suya
**Hausa Name:** Suya
**Category:** Street Food / Delicacy

### Ingredients
- Beef or chicken (thinly sliced)
- Groundnut powder (yaji spice mix)
- Ground pepper
- Ginger
- Salt
- Onion (for garnish)
- Tomatoes (for garnish)

### Preparation
1. Slice the meat into thin, flat strips.
2. Mix the yaji spice mix with salt and ginger.
3. Coat the meat thoroughly with the spice mixture.
4. Thread the meat onto skewers.
5. Allow to marinate briefly.
6. Grill over hot charcoal.
7. Turn regularly to cook evenly.
8. Remove when fully cooked and slightly charred.
9. Garnish with sliced onions and tomatoes.
10. Serve hot.

## Fura da Nono (Millet Balls with Fermented Milk)
**Hausa Name:** Fura da Nono
**Category:** Traditional Drink / Food

### Ingredients
- Millet flour
- Fermented milk (nono)
- Ginger
- Pepper (optional)
- Water

### Preparation
1. Mix millet flour with water and knead into a stiff dough.
2. Shape the dough into small balls.
3. Boil the balls until cooked through.
4. Remove from heat and pound or mash with ginger and spices.
5. Reshape into smooth balls.
6. Place the fura balls in a bowl.
7. Pour fermented milk over the fura.
8. Mash the balls into the milk.
9. Stir until blended to the desired consistency.
10. Serve immediately, chilled or fresh.

## Kilishi (Dried Spiced Meat)
**Hausa Name:** Kilishi
**Category:** Preserved Food / Street Food

### Ingredients
- Lean beef (thinly sliced)
- Groundnut paste
- Ginger
- Onion powder
- Pepper
- Salt
- Spice mix (yaji)

### Preparation
1. Slice beef into thin, flat sheets.
2. Sun-dry the beef until partially dry.
3. Mix groundnut paste with spices and salt.
4. Coat the partially dried beef with the spice paste.
5. Return to the sun to dry completely.
6. Alternatively, grill lightly over low heat.
7. Allow to cool before storing.
8. Store in a dry place.
9. Serve as a snack or alongside meals.

## Kunu (Millet Drink)
**Hausa Name:** Kunu
**Category:** Traditional Drink

### Ingredients
- Millet or sorghum
- Ginger
- Cloves
- Water
- Sugar (optional)

### Preparation
1. Soak the millet or sorghum in water for one to two days.
2. Grind into a smooth paste.
3. Mix with ginger and cloves.
4. Separate a portion and cook as a thick paste.
5. Combine the cooked paste with the raw mixture.
6. Add water and mix thoroughly.
7. Strain through a cloth or sieve.
8. Add sugar if desired.
9. Chill before serving.
10. Serve cold.

## Danwake (Bean Dumplings)
**Hausa Name:** Danwake
**Category:** Staple / Snack

### Ingredients
- Bean flour
- Wheat flour
- Water
- Salt
- Palm oil or groundnut oil (for serving)
- Pepper sauce (for serving)

### Preparation
1. Mix bean flour and wheat flour together.
2. Add salt and water gradually.
3. Knead into a stiff dough.
4. Shape into small cylindrical pieces.
5. Bring water to a boil in a pot.
6. Drop the dough pieces into the boiling water.
7. Cook until the dumplings float and are firm.
8. Drain and arrange in a bowl.
9. Drizzle with oil and pepper sauce.
10. Serve hot.

## Masa (Rice Cakes)
**Hausa Name:** Masa
**Category:** Snack / Street Food

### Ingredients
- Rice
- Yeast or fermented starter
- Water
- Salt
- Sugar (optional)
- Oil (for frying)

### Preparation
1. Soak rice in water overnight.
2. Grind into a smooth paste.
3. Allow to ferment with yeast for several hours.
4. Add salt and sugar if desired.
5. Heat a round-molded pan (masa pan) with oil.
6. Pour batter into each mold.
7. Allow to cook until set on one side.
8. Flip and cook the other side.
9. Remove when golden.
10. Serve hot with honey, sugar, or soup.


# Fashion (*Sawa*)

## Clothing

### Babban Riga (Grand Robe)
**Hausa Name:** Babban Riga
**Category:** Traditional Men's Clothing

#### Description
The *babban riga* is a loose-fitting, embroidered ceremonial robe worn by Hausa men. It is one of the most prestigious items of traditional male attire in northern Nigeria, also known as *agbada* in broader Nigerian usage. The robe flows to the ankles and is typically embroidered at the neckline and chest with intricate patterns. It is worn over an inner tunic and trousers. The babban riga represents formality, dignity, and social standing. During the 2015 Nigerian presidential election, candidates wore culturally significant clothing including the babban riga to send political messages to different voting communities, demonstrating the continued cultural significance of traditional dress.

#### Materials
- Fine cotton or silk fabric
- Embroidery thread
- Inner tunic fabric
- Matching trousers fabric

#### How It Is Worn
1. The inner tunic (*kaftani*) is worn first.
2. Matching trousers are worn underneath.
3. The babban riga is draped over the body.
4. The excess fabric is arranged elegantly over one shoulder.
5. A cap (*hula*) is worn on the head.
6. Leather sandals complete the attire.

#### Who Wears It
- Adult men
- Elders
- Emirate officials
- Grooms
- Community leaders

#### Occasions
- Religious celebrations (Eid)
- Traditional ceremonies
- Weddings
- Formal community gatherings
- Political occasions

#### Cultural Significance
- Represents formality and respect.
- Indicates social standing and wealth.
- Associated with Islamic cultural identity.
- Preserves traditional Hausa craftsmanship.

#### Modern Usage
The babban riga remains the standard formal attire for Hausa men at important ceremonies and is widely worn across northern Nigeria.

### Hijab (*Mayafi*)
**Hausa Name:** Mayafi
**Category:** Traditional Women's Clothing

#### Description
The hijab is worn by Muslim Hausa women as an expression of Islamic modesty. In northern Nigeria, women are generally expected to cover their hair and body when in public. The style and extent of covering varies by community and individual practice. In 2016, a Nigerian court ruling granted Muslim women the right to wear the hijab in public schools in two southwestern Nigerian states, highlighting the ongoing cultural and legal significance of Islamic dress.

#### Materials
- Cotton fabric
- Chiffon
- Silk (for special occasions)
- Lace (for ceremonies)

#### How It Is Worn
1. Hair is covered completely.
2. The cloth is wrapped around the head and secured.
3. The neck and chest are covered.
4. A full-length outer garment may accompany it.
5. Hands and face may or may not be covered depending on personal practice and community norms.

#### Who Wears It
- Muslim women
- Girls of appropriate age
- Visitors to mosques

#### Occasions
- Daily wear
- Religious observances
- Mosque visits
- Formal gatherings

#### Cultural Significance
- Symbolizes Islamic faith and modesty.
- Represents religious identity.
- Reflects adherence to Islamic values in public life.

#### Modern Usage
The hijab is widely worn across northern Nigeria and is a central element of Hausa women's dress.

### Zane (Women's Wrapper)
**Hausa Name:** Zane
**Category:** Traditional Women's Clothing

#### Description
The *zane* is a traditional wrapper worn by Hausa women. It is a rectangular piece of fabric draped around the lower body. Women often wear multiple layers, pairing the zane with a blouse and a head covering. The zane is worn for daily activities, market visits, family gatherings, and ceremonies. Decorative zane fabrics are reserved for special occasions.

#### Materials
- Cotton fabric
- Locally dyed textiles
- Imported fabrics

#### How It Is Worn
1. The fabric is wrapped around the waist.
2. One end is tucked in securely.
3. A blouse is worn on the upper body.
4. A head covering is added.
5. Decorative jewelry may complete the outfit.

#### Who Wears It
- Women of all ages
- Married women
- Market traders
- Rural and urban women alike

#### Occasions
- Daily activities
- Market visits
- Family events
- Religious observances

#### Cultural Significance
- Represents womanhood and dignity.
- Reflects cultural identity.
- Serves both practical and ceremonial purposes.

#### Modern Usage
The zane remains a widely worn garment throughout Hausa communities.

## Traditional Hausa Caps (*Hula*)

### Meaning and Description
Caps are an essential part of Hausa male identity and dress. Various styles of embroidered caps are worn to indicate social status, occasion, and religious observance. The wearing of a cap is considered important for Muslim men, particularly during prayer and formal occasions.

#### Materials
- Cotton
- Wool
- Embroidered fabric

#### How It Is Worn
1. The cap is placed firmly on the head.
2. It is paired with a babban riga or other formal attire.
3. Different cap styles indicate different occasions or social ranks.
4. Worn during prayer, ceremonies, and daily life.

#### Who Wears It
- Adult men
- Elders
- Religious leaders
- Emirate officials

#### Occasions
- Daily wear
- Prayer
- Ceremonies and festivals

#### Cultural Significance
- Reflects Islamic identity.
- Indicates social and religious standing.
- Completes the formal Hausa male appearance.

#### Modern Usage
Embroidered caps remain universally worn by Hausa men across northern Nigeria.

## Hair

Hair covering is an essential element of Hausa women's identity, closely tied to Islamic values of modesty. In traditional Hausa society, elaborate braided hairstyles were worn by women beneath their head coverings. These styles communicated:
- Age and marital status
- Social standing
- Community identity
- Participation in ceremonies

Traditional hairstyles were often maintained with natural oils and thread. Hairstyling was a social activity, with women gathering to braid and decorate one another's hair. Some elaborate styles could take many hours to complete.

## Traditional Hausa Jewelry and Accessories

### Meaning and Description
Jewelry and accessories in Hausa culture are markers of beauty, wealth, and status. Women wear beads, bangles, and earrings, while men carry prayer beads (*tasbih*) as accessories. For Hausa women, elaborate jewelry is reserved for weddings and festivals.

### Common Accessories

#### Glass and Metal Bangles
Bangles worn on the wrists are common everyday accessories for Hausa women. For special occasions, more elaborate sets are worn.

#### Beaded Necklaces
Beaded necklaces are worn for ceremonial occasions, particularly at weddings and festivals. Different colors and patterns can carry symbolic meaning within different communities.

#### Anklets
Anklets for women and girls are worn during celebrations and special occasions.

#### Prayer Beads (*Tasbih*)
Muslim men commonly carry prayer beads used for reciting the names and praises of God. These beads are both a religious tool and a cultural accessory.

#### Embroidered Leather Goods
Embroidered leather bags, wallets, and shoes are important craft products of Hausa artisans and are worn and carried by both men and women.

### Cultural Significance
- Enhances beauty and reflects cultural identity.
- Marks important life events such as weddings.
- Demonstrates wealth and social standing.
- Reflects the Islamic aesthetic of elegance without ostentation.

#### Modern Usage
Jewelry and accessories remain an important part of Hausa fashion, particularly for women at celebrations and weddings. Hausa leatherwork and embroidery are renowned across West Africa.
